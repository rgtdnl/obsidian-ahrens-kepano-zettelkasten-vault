# How to Take Smart Notes in Obsidian — A Practical Guide

*Based on "How to Take Smart Notes" by Sönke Ahrens (2022 revised edition)*
https://www.amazon.com/How-Take-Smart-Notes-Technique-ebook/dp/B09V5M8FR5

*The Zettelkasten method, as practiced by sociologist Niklas Luhmann, who wrote 58 books and hundreds of articles using this system.*

---

## The Big Picture: Why Note-Taking Matters More Than You Think

The core insight of this book is simple but radical: **writing is not what follows reading, learning, and thinking — it *is* the medium of all three.** Most people struggle with writing not because they lack talent or willpower, but because they treat note-taking as a trivial side task. In reality, the quality of your final output is determined almost entirely by how you take notes in the weeks, months, and years *before* you face the blank page.

Niklas Luhmann was a civil servant with no sociology degree who became one of the 20th century's most productive and influential social scientists. His secret wasn't genius — it was a deceptively simple note-taking system called the **Zettelkasten** (slip-box). He wrote ~6 notes a day for decades, amassing 90,000 notes that became a thinking partner, idea generator, and productivity engine.

The system works because it **externalizes thinking** — it takes the burden of remembering off your brain so your brain can focus on what it does best: making connections, generating insight, and being creative.

## The Four Underlying Principles

### Principle 1: Writing Is the Only Thing That Matters

Studying, research, and learning are not preparation for writing — they *are* writing. Every lecture, discussion, reading, and idea should be accompanied by a pen in your hand. If you make writing the center of everything you do, everything else improves: you read more engagedly, listen more critically, and think more clearly.

> "If you want to learn something for the long run, you have to write it down. If you want to really understand something, you have to translate it into your own words."

**In practice:** Don't separate "taking notes" from "doing the work." They're the same activity. Every idea you encounter should be processed through writing.

### Principle 2: Simplicity Is Paramount

The system's power comes from its simplicity, not despite it. Like the shipping container — a simple box that revolutionized global trade — the Zettelkasten works because it standardizes everything into one format and eliminates decisions about where things go.

**In practice:** Don't collect a dozen different note-taking apps, methods, and formats. Use one tool, one format, one system. The fewer decisions you make about *how* to take notes, the more mental energy you have for *what* the notes are about.

### Principle 3: Nobody Ever Starts From Scratch

The "blank page" is a myth. If you've been taking smart notes, you never sit down to write with nothing — you sit down with a collection of already-developed ideas, arguments, and references that you've been building for months. Topics and research questions *emerge* from your notes rather than being decided upfront.

> "The problem of finding a topic is replaced by the problem of having too many topics to write about."

**In practice:** Don't brainstorm for a topic. Look into your slip-box and see where clusters of notes have built up — those are your topics, already supported by material.

### Principle 4: Let the Work Carry You Forward

A good workflow creates a **virtuous circle**: the work itself becomes rewarding, which motivates you to continue, which makes you better at it, which makes it more enjoyable. You shouldn't need willpower to work — you should need willpower to *stop*.

This happens when:
- Each task is small enough to complete in one sitting
- You get immediate feedback (writing reveals gaps in understanding)
- You can switch between tasks freely when you get stuck
- The system grows more useful over time

## The Note Types

This distinction is crucial. Confusing these categories is the #1 reason note-taking systems fail.

Ahrens describes three core types (fleeting, literature, permanent) plus project notes as a separate category that never enters the slip-box. Structure notes are a community addition popularised by the Obsidian/Zettelkasten community, not part of the book. Archive is where finished project material goes.

| Type | Purpose | Lifespan | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fleeting notes** | Quick reminders of ideas while you're busy | Discarded within 1-2 days | Inbox / notebook |
| **Literature notes** | Ideas from sources, translated into your own words | Kept in the reference system | Reference manager (e.g. Zotero) + `10 - Literature/` |
| **Permanent notes** | Self-contained ideas, written as if for publication | Forever | The slip-box (Zettelkasten) |
| **Project notes** | Notes relevant to one specific project | Discarded or archived when project ends | Project folder |
| **Structure notes** | A map of links to notes on one topic | As long as the topic stays active | Zettelkasten (as MOCs) |

### Fleeting Notes
These are throwaway reminders. You're in a meeting, walking down the street, or reading a book, and an idea pops up. Jot it down quickly — don't worry about format, spelling, or completeness. The only requirement: collect them in **one place** (a notebook, a single inbox note in Obsidian) and process them within a day or two.

> ⚠️ Fleeting notes are **not** the final form of the idea. They're just reminders. If you don't turn them into permanent notes quickly, you'll forget what they meant.

### Clippings and Raw Literature Notes (inbox contents)
The inbox holds three kinds of material, all **flat in one folder with no subfolders** — the point of keeping them visible is that *everything visible is everything still needing processing*. Once processed, they leave the inbox permanently:

- **Clippings** — web clipper captures (articles, podcasts, YouTube videos). Process into permanent notes, then **delete the clipping**. The original is one click away on the web; keeping the clipping after extraction is hoarding.
- **Raw literature notes** — verbatim text from a source (highlights, quotes, passages) that has not yet been translated into your own words. These are a staging area, not a final form. Process them into proper literature notes (translated into your own words), then **delete the raw note**. Do NOT archive them — the book itself is the source of truth, and archiving untranslated raw text just fills the archive with dead weight. (User decision 2026-08-04: delete after extraction, both clippings and raw literature notes.)
- **Fleeting notes** — as above; discarded after processing.

### Literature Notes
When you read, you write short notes about the content: what you don't want to forget, what might be useful for your own thinking, always translated into your own words (never copied quotes). These live in your reference system with the bibliographic details, and are the raw material for permanent notes.

### Permanent Notes
These are the heart of the system. A permanent note:
- Contains **one idea** per note
- Is written in **full sentences** as if for someone else
- Is **self-contained** — understandable even when you've forgotten the original context
- Includes **references** to sources
- Is as **precise, clear, and brief** as possible

Writing a permanent note forces you to think: *Does this contradict, correct, support, or add to what I already have? Can I combine ideas to generate something new? What questions does this trigger?*

### Project Notes
Anything relevant to a specific writing project: outlines, drafts, to-do lists, comments on the manuscript, collected references. These live in a project-specific folder and can be discarded (or archived) when the project is done. They should **never** be mixed into the slip-box itself.

> The book's words: project notes "can be discarded or archived after the project is finished. Only if the notes of these three categories are kept separated will it be possible to build a critical mass of ideas within the slip-box."

### Structure Notes (community addition, not in the book)
A structure note is a note whose content is mostly **links to other notes** on one topic, each with a one-line annotation. It has no original idea of its own; it is a *map*, not a container.

- **Purpose:** navigation. When a cluster of permanent notes grows past what you can see in one glance, a structure note gives you the overview.
- **When to use:** when you keep searching for "everything I have on X." That is the signal the cluster needs a structure note.
- **What it is NOT:** a filing cabinet, a topic folder, or a place ideas go to die.
- In Obsidian terms, these are your **Maps of Content (MOCs)** — see "Entry/overview notes" under Managing Connections below.
- **Honest caveat:** Luhmann's original system worked without them. Adopt structure notes when you feel the pain of lost clusters, not preemptively.

### Archive (the bin for the indecisive)
Ahrens' exact phrase: "in an archive — the bin for the indecisive."

- **What it is:** the home for finished **project notes** you can't quite bring yourself to throw away, for the "maybe I'll need this later" feeling.
- **Where:** `40 - Archive/`, one subfolder per finished project. Clippings and raw literature notes do NOT go here — they are deleted after processing (see above).
- **The critical rule:** **permanent notes never go to archive.** They live in the slip-box forever; archiving one breaks the system. Archive is only for project material.
- **Don't confuse two uses of the word:** "archive" also appears in the book for filing literature notes into your reference system (Zotero). That is a different operation entirely.
- **Practical effect of separation:** because finished project folders move out of `30 - Projects/`, the active project space stays clean and the slip-box never gets diluted by project-specific noise.

## The Four Tools You Need

1. **Something to write with and on** — pen and paper, a phone app, anything that lets you capture ideas with zero friction. This is for fleeting notes.
2. **A reference management system** — Zotero (free, recommended) or similar. Stores bibliographic references and your literature notes together.
3. **The slip-box (Zettelkasten)** — your permanent note collection. In this guide, that's **Obsidian**.
4. **An editor** — whatever you write your final manuscripts in (Word, Google Docs, LaTeX). Just make sure your reference manager integrates with it.

> "More is unnecessary, less is impossible."

## Setting Up the Zettelkasten in Obsidian

### Folder Structure

Keep it minimal:

```
📁 00 - Inbox/          ← FLAT, no subfolders: clippings + fleeting notes + raw literature notes
                            (visibility is the point: everything visible = everything needing processing)
📁 10 - Literature/     ← Literature notes (flat; reference integrated into the template)
📁 20 - Slip-box/       ← Permanent notes + index/MOCs (the heart of the system)
📁 30 - Projects/       ← One subfolder per writing project
📁 40 - Archive/        ← Finished project folders only ("the bin for the indecisive")
📁 50 - Templates/      ← Note templates
```

### Key Obsidian Features to Enable

- **Backlinks** — built in, this is Obsidian's superpower and the digital equivalent of Luhmann's cross-references
- **Search** — fast full-text search across all notes
- **Graph View** — visualize your note connections (helps spot clusters)
- **Tags** — use sparingly, as entry points rather than a filing system
- **Templates** (core plugin) — create templates for literature notes and permanent notes
- **Daily Notes** (core plugin) — use as your inbox for fleeting notes

### Note Naming Convention

Luhmann used numbered IDs (21/3d7a7) to create note sequences. In Obsidian, you have two options:

1. **Time-stamped IDs** (recommended): `202608021430` — unique, sortable, and easy to generate. Use the Templater plugin or manually type `{{date:YYYYMMDDHHmm}}`.
2. **Title-based**: Use a descriptive title as the filename. Linking is by `[[title]]`.

Either way, the note's filename is its permanent identifier. Don't rename notes once they're linked to others.

### Note Templates

**Literature note template:**
```markdown
---
source: 
author: 
year: 
type: literature
---

# {{title}}

**Reference:** 

**Key points:**
- 

**Quotes worth keeping:**
> 

**My thoughts:**
- 
```

**Permanent note template:**
```markdown
---
type: permanent
created: {{date}}
---

# {{title}}

[Write your idea here in full sentences, as if explaining to someone else.]

**Source:** [[literature note link]] or [[another permanent note]]

**Related:** [[note]] [[note]]
```

## The Workflow: Step by Step

### Step 1: Make Fleeting Notes
Throughout the day, whenever an idea strikes — in conversation, while reading, in the shower — write it down. Don't think about format. Just capture it in your notebook or Daily Note in Obsidian. These are reminders, not polished thoughts.

> 💡 Keep a small notebook with you at all times. It's hard to beat pen and paper for simplicity.

### Step 2: Make Literature Notes
When you read something, take notes about the content. Write down what you don't want to forget or what might be useful for your own thinking.

**How to read with a pen:**
- **Don't just copy quotes.** Translate ideas into your own words. Copying lets you skip understanding; rephrasing forces you to think.
- **Be extremely selective.** Don't capture everything — only what resonates and what you might use.
- **Reflect on frames and assumptions.** Ask: What is *not* mentioned? What distinction is being made? What would have to be true for this argument to hold?
- **Consider handwriting.** Studies show handwriting improves understanding because it's slower, forcing you to focus on the gist rather than copying verbatim.

Store literature notes in your `10 - Literature/` folder with the reference details.

### Step 3: Make Permanent Notes
This is the critical step. Ideally **once a day**, go through your fleeting and literature notes and think about how they relate to your own thinking and what's already in your slip-box.

**The process:**
1. Review your fleeting notes and literature notes from the day
2. For each idea worth keeping, write a **new permanent note** in `20 - Slip-box/`
3. Ask: *Does this contradict, correct, support, or add to what I already have? Can I combine ideas? What questions does this trigger?*
4. Write in **full sentences** as if for someone else — disclose sources, make references, be precise and brief
5. **One idea per note**
6. Throw away the fleeting notes; file the literature notes in your reference system

**The key question is NOT "under which topic do I store this?" but "in which context will I want to stumble upon it again?"**

### Step 4: Add Notes to the Slip-Box
1. **File each new note behind a related note** it directly follows up on. In Obsidian, this means creating a conceptual sequence — link the new note to the note it continues, and vice versa.
2. **Add links to other related notes.** Use `[[wikilinks]]` to connect ideas across the slip-box.
3. **Make sure it can be found.** Either link to it from an index/entry note, or from a note that's already connected to your index.

> 🎯 **Write new notes in response to existing notes whenever possible.** This is what turns a collection of isolated notes into a thinking partner. Instead of hoarding snippets, you're continuing a dialogue.

### Step 5: Develop Topics Bottom-Up
Don't decide what to write about. Instead, let topics emerge from your slip-box. After a while, you'll notice clusters of notes building up around certain themes — those are your topics, and they come with ready-made material.

Look for:
- Long chains of notes (developed arguments)
- Clusters of connected notes (rich topics)
- Gaps and open questions (research directions)

Read more to challenge, strengthen, or change your arguments. Take more notes. Follow your interest.

### Step 6: Assemble and Draft
When you're ready to write:
1. Collect all relevant notes for the topic into a project folder
2. Copy them into your manuscript / outliner
3. Bring them into a logical order
4. Look for what's missing and what's redundant
5. Turn the notes into a continuous text — don't just copy; translate and embed them into your argument
6. Edit and proofread

> "The only thing left to do is revise this rough draft and send it off."

### Step 7: Review
- Delete what has no function ("kill your darlings") — but save cut material in a "rest" file; you might use it elsewhere
- Check for holes in the argument
- Fill gaps or change the argument

## How to Write Good Permanent Notes

### The Translation Principle
Don't copy ideas from their original context. **Translate** them into the context of your own thinking. This means using your own words, your own frameworks, and your own questions — while staying true to the original meaning.

Copying quotes strips them of context and changes their meaning. Translating forces understanding.

### Write for Your Future (Ignorant) Self
Your future self will have forgotten everything about the context. Write each note so it stands on its own. Full sentences. Clear references. No shorthand only you-understand-right-now.

### One Idea Per Note
This is non-negotiable. If a note contains multiple ideas, you can't recombine them freely later. One idea per note is the precondition for free assembly.

> 💡 **Rule of thumb:** Each note should fit on one screen without scrolling.

### Ask Elaborating Questions
Every time you write a note, ask:
- What does this mean for my own research/thinking?
- How does it connect to what I already have?
- What is *not* mentioned? What is excluded?
- Is this similar to something else? Different? How?
- What questions does this trigger?

### Link, Don't Sort
The slip-box is not organized by topic. It's organized by **connections**. Topics and themes emerge bottom-up from the network of links. This is what makes the slip-box *more* useful as it grows, rather than less.

**Think like a writer, not an archivist:**
- The archivist asks: "Which keyword is most fitting?"
- The writer asks: "In which circumstances will I want to stumble upon this note, even if I forget about it?"

## Managing Connections in Obsidian

### Four Types of Links (Luhmann's system, adapted for digital):

1. **Entry/overview notes** — Notes linked from your index that give an overview of a topic. Collect links to all relevant notes on a topic with a brief annotation for each. These are your "Maps of Content" (MOCs).

2. **Note-to-note links** — The most common and important type. Direct links between two related notes, regardless of where they are in the system. These create surprising cross-connections.

3. **Follow-up links** — When a new note directly continues or comments on an existing note, link them as a sequence. This builds "chains of thought."

4. **Index links** — Your index is a simple note (or a few notes) with links to 1-2 entry points per topic. Keep it sparse — most notes are found through other notes, not through the index.

### Using Tags
Tags in Obsidian should be used **sparingly** — as entry points, not as a filing system. Good tags describe the *context in which you'd want to find this note*, not the *topic of the note itself*.

**Bad tags** (archivist thinking): `#psychology`, `#experiment`, `#misjudgment`
**Good tags** (writer thinking): `#decision-making`, `#capital-allocation`, `#election-strategies`

> The best keywords are usually *not* words that appear in the note itself.

### Using Backlinks
Obsidian's backlinks panel shows every note that links to the current note. This is your automatic "what else is connected to this?" view. Check it whenever you open a note — it often surfaces forgotten connections.

## Key Habits and Mindset Shifts

### 1. Read With a Pen in Hand
Always. If you read without writing, it's "almost as valuable as not having read at all." Writing while reading forces you to:
- Check your understanding (can you rephrase it?)
- Be selective (you can't write down everything)
- Think beyond the text (what does this mean for your questions?)

### 2. Process Fleeting Notes Daily
Review your fleeting notes **within a day or two** and turn the good ones into permanent notes. If you wait too long, you'll forget what they meant or they'll seem banal.

### 3. Don't Plan — Become an Expert
Planning is the enemy of insight. You can't plan for ideas you haven't had yet. Instead of following a rigid multi-step plan, develop the intuition to know what task is most promising right now.

> "Real experts don't make plans."

This comes from experience and feedback loops — the small, daily practice of taking notes, making connections, and seeing what works.

### 4. Separate Tasks — Give Each the Right Attention
Writing involves many different tasks: reading, understanding, reflecting, connecting, outlining, drafting, editing, proofreading. Each requires a **different kind of attention**:
- **Reading/understanding**: focused, analytical
- **Making connections**: playful, associative, broad
- **Outlining**: big-picture, structural
- **Drafting**: flowing, generative — let the inner critic wait
- **Proofreading**: critical, detail-oriented, detached

Don't try to do them simultaneously. The slip-box naturally separates them into distinct, completable tasks.

### 5. Work on Multiple Projects Simultaneously
When you get stuck on one project, switch to another. Luhmann always worked on multiple manuscripts at once and never experienced writer's block. The slip-box feeds all of them — ideas generated while reading for one project often become material for another.

> "I never force myself to do anything I don't feel like. Whenever I am stuck, I do something else."

### 6. Get Closure on Each Task
Each small task (writing one note, making one connection, reviewing one paragraph) should be **completable in one sitting**. This uses the Zeigarnik effect to your advantage: open tasks occupy your mind, but writing them down (completing or recording them) frees that mental space.

### 7. Reduce Decisions
By standardizing your format, your tools, and your workflow, you eliminate countless micro-decisions. Every decision you don't have to make is mental energy saved for actual thinking.

### 8. Seek Disconfirming Information
The slip-box naturally combats confirmation bias because:
- You develop ideas bottom-up, not from a preconceived hypothesis
- Disconfirming data is *attractive* — it creates more connections and discussion in the slip-box
- You're collecting relevant information regardless of which argument it supports

> "If insight becomes a threat to your academic or writing success, you are doing it wrong."

### 9. Embrace Restrictions
The standardized format (one idea per note, plain text, one tool) is a *feature*, not a bug. Creativity thrives under constraints. Haikus, chess, and binary code are all powerful because of — not despite — their restrictions.

## The Zeigarnik Effect: Getting Things Out of Your Head

Open, unfinished tasks occupy your short-term memory and drain mental resources. But you don't have to *finish* a task to free that space — you just have to **write it down** in a way that convinces your brain it will be taken care of.

This is why the slip-box works: by externalizing every thought, connection, and open question, you empty your working memory and can focus completely on the task in front of you.

> "The brain doesn't distinguish between an actual finished task and one that is postponed by taking a note."

## Learning by Not Trying

The slip-box is a learning system, not just a writing system. Here's why it works:

1. **Elaboration** — Translating ideas into your own words and connecting them to existing knowledge is the most effective learning method known to research.

2. **Retrieval practice** — Every time you search the slip-box or make connections, you're retrieving information, which strengthens memory.

3. **Spacing and variation** — You encounter the same notes at different times and in different contexts, which deepens understanding.

4. **Forgetting as a feature** — By offloading facts to the slip-box, you free your brain to focus on understanding and patterns. The slip-box remembers the details so you can remember the *gist*.

> "Learned right — which means understanding, which means connecting in a meaningful way to previous knowledge — information almost cannot be forgotten anymore."

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Treating All Notes as Permanent
If you put fleeting notes or project notes into the slip-box, the good ideas get diluted by noise. Keep the categories strictly separated.

### 2. Collecting Only for Specific Projects
If you only collect notes for the project at hand, you start from scratch every time. The slip-box is a **permanent reservoir** that grows across projects.

### 3. Organizing by Topic
Topic-based filing systems get messier as they grow. The slip-box gets *more* useful as it grows because it's organized by connections, not categories.

### 4. Copying Quotes Instead of Translating
Quotes stripped of context lose their meaning. Always translate into your own words — it's the only way to ensure understanding.

### 5. Over-Highlighting
Underlining sentences and making margin notes are just **fleeting notes**. If you don't process them into permanent notes, they're useless.

### 6. Waiting for the Perfect System
Don't spend weeks setting up plugins, themes, and workflows. Start taking notes today. The system reveals what it needs as you use it.

### 7. Planning Instead of Doing
The biggest threat to productivity is the illusion that you can plan your way to good writing. You can't. You can only build up a critical mass of good notes and let topics emerge.

## Quick Start: Your First Week

### Day 1: Set Up
- Download Obsidian and create a vault
- Create the folder structure (00 Inbox, 10 Literature, 20 Slip-box, 30 Projects, 40 Archive, 50 Templates)
- Enable core plugins: Search, Backlinks, Templates, Daily Notes
- Create templates for literature notes and permanent notes

### Day 2: Start Reading With a Pen
- Pick something you're already reading
- Take literature notes: write down key ideas in your own words
- Include the bibliographic reference
- File in `10 - Literature/`

### Day 3: Write Your First Permanent Notes
- Review your literature notes from Day 2
- For each idea worth keeping, write a permanent note
- One idea per note, full sentences, self-contained
- Link to related notes (even if there aren't any yet — you'll build connections over time)
- File in `20 - Slip-box/`

### Day 4-5: Build the Habit
- Continue reading and taking literature notes
- Write 3-6 permanent notes per day (Luhmann's average was 6)
- Each time you add a note, search your slip-box for related notes and add links
- Process your fleeting notes daily

### Day 6: Review Your Clusters
- Open Obsidian's Graph View
- Look for clusters of connected notes
- These are emergent topics — potential writing projects

### Day 7: Draft Something Small
- Pick a cluster that interests you
- Assemble the relevant notes into a project folder
- Arrange them in a logical order
- Write a rough draft by turning the notes into a continuous text
- Don't worry about perfection — it's a first draft

## The Zettelkasten vs. PARA (Building a Second Brain)

If you've read Tiago Forte's "Building a Second Brain," here's how the two systems compare:

| Aspect | Zettelkasten (Ahrens) | PARA (Forte) |
|---|---|---|
| **Organizing principle** | Connections between notes | Actionability (Projects → Areas → Resources → Archives) |
| **Note format** | One idea per note, atomic | Notes can contain multiple ideas |
| **Distillation** | Translating into own words + linking | Progressive Summarization (bold → highlight → summarize) |
| **Topics** | Emerge bottom-up from clusters | Decided upfront (projects) |
| **Primary tool** | Links and backlinks | Folders and tags |
| **Focus** | Thinking, learning, writing | Productivity, project execution |
| **Best for** | Knowledge workers, academics, writers | General productivity, project management |

**They're complementary.** Many people use PARA for project/area management and Zettelkasten for their permanent knowledge notes. Obsidian supports both approaches.

## The Mindset: From Planner to Expert

The deepest lesson of the book is a shift in identity:

- **Planners** follow rigid step-by-step plans, push through with willpower, and are glad when it's over.
- **Experts** develop intuition through practice and feedback, follow their interest, and can't stop because the work itself is rewarding.

The Zettelkasten doesn't tell you what to do at each stage. Instead, it gives you a structure of clearly separable tasks, each completable in one sitting, each providing instant feedback through the act of writing and connecting. Over time, you develop the intuition to know which task is most promising right now — and that's what expertise looks like.

> "I only do what is easy. I only write when I immediately know how to do it. If I falter for a moment, I put the matter aside and do something else." — Niklas Luhmann
